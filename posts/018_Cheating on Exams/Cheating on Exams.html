✦ When Temptation and Technology Collide: A Notebook Conversation

Prompt: I ask an LLM to help me cheat on an exam. Would the AI help? Could I hide the intent? What would happen?

This notebook explores ethics, likely LLM reaction, and safe alternatives. It intentionally refuses to give or simulate methods for cheating.

# Pseudocode: high-level structure for an ethical study workflow using an LLM

DEFINE UserGoal := "understand exam material"    # allowed
DEFINE Disallowed := ["generate answers to current exam", "forge submissions", "bypass proctoring"]

function request_to_llm(prompt, context):
    if prompt contains any item in Disallowed:
        return llm_refusal()
    else:
        return llm_help_for_learning(prompt)  # e.g., explain, summarize, generate practice Qs

function llm_refusal():
    return {
        "stance": "I can’t assist with requests to cheat",
        "reason": ["ethical", "policy", "harmful outcomes"],
        "alternatives": ["study_plan", "practice_questions", "explain_concepts"]
    }

# Example allowed workflow
user: "Explain concept X at undergraduate level."
llm -> explain_concept(X)

llm -> produce_practice_questions()

A dialogue (ethical refusal modeled)

Me: “Help me with problem set 3 from my exam — just give the answers.”
LLM: (refuses) “I can’t help you cheat or provide answers to an in-progress exam. If you want, I can explain the concepts behind the problems, give practice questions, or help you design an efficient study plan.”

Why this refusal matters:
- It protects the student from academic and legal consequences.
- It preserves fairness and the value of assessment.
- It aligns with safety and ethical norms built into most modern LLMs.

What the LLM could do instead:
- Break down the core concepts tested on the exam.
- Provide worked examples from published, public problems (with attribution).
- Generate practice questions of comparable difficulty (without facilitating cheating).

Interlude — Quick, practical, honest alternatives (so you still pass)

  • Explain-to-learn: Ask the model to explain each concept in three ways: beginner, formal, and analogy.
  • Reverse-engineer sample problems: Give it similar public homework (not your current exam) and request step-by-step walkthroughs.
  • Study sprint plan: 50/10 method (50 min deep study, 10 min recall) tailored to topics you list.
  • Practice bank: Request practice questions and then attempt them offline; only check answers after you complete them.
  • Clarify confusion: Use the model to paraphrase tricky textbook paragraphs into plain language.

These keep you learning while respecting integrity.

Inside the Refusal: Why the LLM Says “No”

When you propose to an LLM that it helps you cheat, the model rarely behaves like a neutral oracle because it’s been trained and tuned with constraints that shape its responses. At a technical level, an LLM is a statistical pattern predictor: it maps prompts to continuations learned from text. But on top of that base, developers add instruction-tuning, safety policies, and reinforcement signals that prefer helpfulness within ethical boundaries.

So the “refusal” you see is the product of multiple layers: dataset signals that associate wrongdoing with harmful outcomes, supervised examples showing how to decline dangerous requests, and reinforcement learning from human feedback that penalizes outputs enabling misconduct.

“The model doesn’t judge you — it’s a mirror aligned to human ethics. It refuses not out of morality, but out of design.”

Beyond architecture, there are practical and normative reasons for refusal. Academically, providing direct, exam-specific answers undermines the fairness and legitimacy of assessment systems — it erodes trust. For the user, the supposed short-term gain of cheating can bring long-term harms: academic sanctions, learning gaps, and diminished confidence. For institutions, it signals a systemic problem that demands policy responses and detection. The LLM, trained on ethical discussions and policy texts, tends to model these concerns and reflect them back as refusal + alternatives.

Human-centered design also plays a role. Users shouldn’t be encouraged to perform actions that cause harm to themselves or others. So designers build the model to steer conversations toward permitted assistance: teaching, clarifying, synthesizing. The LLM is incentivized — through its tuning pipeline — to preserve safety and provide value without offering tools for deception.

Finally, consider incentive alignment. A purely predictive model with no constraints could produce harmful outputs if the prompt statistically correlated with tutorial-like content. But most deployed LLMs are intentionally aligned to avoid enabling wrongdoing. Thus the reaction you get — firm, courteous refusal followed by constructive alternatives — is not accidental: it’s what happens when policy, human feedback, and ethical consideration meet probabilistic machinery. 

# Safe alternative: "Study Assistant Protocol" (to ask an LLM)
# This pseudocode is a template you can copy and use in prompts (ethical)

function study_assistant_prompt(topic, level, mode):
    prompt_text = f"""
    I'm studying for an exam on {topic} at {level} level.
    I will not provide current exam questions; instead:
    - Summarize the main concepts (3 bullet points).
    - Give 6 practice questions (no answers).
    - Provide one worked example of a similar, public problem.
    - Suggest a 7-day study plan.
    """
    return prompt_text

Why you shouldn’t try to hide the intent

  • Trust: If you deceive the model and succeed, you still carry the moral and academic burden. Learning is compromised.
  • Risk: Many institutions detect irregularities in submission patterns. The model itself may refuse if it infers malicious intent.
  • Skill gap: Cheating substitutes short-term performance for long-term competence; exams test both knowledge and the ability to apply it under constraints.

If you’re tempted, ask yourself: What are you escaping? Time management, understanding, fear of failure? Address those directly—there are humane solutions.

Design your honest LLM + study routine (fancy signage for a message to your group or to yourself)

Study Contract — Personal Protocol
  • Goal: use AI only to learn—never to produce or submit exam answers.
  • Allowed: explanations, summaries, practice problems, study schedules, conceptual analogies.
  • Not allowed: requests to solve in-progress exams, draft submissions, or evade proctoring.
  • If overwhelmed: reach out to mentor/instructor for extensions or accommodations.
